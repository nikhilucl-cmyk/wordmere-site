Our retention magic number was a mirage

Players who cleared six levels in their first session came back at roughly double the rate. So we made more players clear six levels. Retention did not move at all.

28 October 2026 · The Wordmere team

Every product eventually finds a magic number. Ours was six levels.

Plot how likely a new player is to come back the next day against how many levels they cleared in their very first session, and the chart is almost too tidy. It is flat and low up to about five. Somewhere between five and six it roughly doubles. It keeps climbing after that.

About half of our new players were finishing their first session in the flat part of that curve. The brief writes itself: get more of them past five.

So we did

We went at first-session depth directly. The opening levels were made quicker to read and quicker to clear. Interruptions before the threshold were taken out. The moment a brand-new player finds their first word was redesigned so that the thing the game is asking of them cannot be missed.

It worked, in the sense that it did what it was supposed to do. In one release, the share of new players clearing six or more levels on their first day went up by about a third.

Day-one retention did not move. Not slightly — it sat exactly where it had been sitting for two months, and it is sitting there still.

The threshold was real. It was never a cause. We moved the symptom and the illness did not notice.

Why the number lied

A player who clears six levels in one sitting has told you something about themselves before your level design gets a vote. They were in the mood. The game landed. They had twenty minutes and nothing better to do with them. Depth was a measurement of that intent, and intent is what comes back tomorrow.

Make the levels faster and you move the measurement. You have not been anywhere near the thing it was measuring.

This is the oldest trap in product analytics and it still catches everybody, because the correlation is strong, the threshold is sharp, and shipping against a proxy feels exactly like shipping against the real thing. “Players who do X retain better” is never a finding. It is an invitation to ask the actual question, which is: and if we make them do X?

The cheap way to ask it, before you build anything: find a change you have already shipped that moved the proxy, and check whether the outcome followed. If no such change exists in your history, you do not know yet, and that is worth saying out loud before a quarter of roadmap gets built on top of it.

What the churn actually looked like

The deeper mistake was treating early churn as one thing. It is two, with opposite causes, and the bigger one is invisible if you go looking for difficulty.

The early group is not stuck. They leave around twenty seconds into a level. Nobody is defeated at twenty seconds. Almost everyone who starts the first level finishes it, so the first level is not hard. And they leave with their hints unspent — they do not exhaust the help we gave them and give up, they never reach for it once. There is also a slice of installs, the single largest hole in our funnel, that opens the app, registers a screen view, and never starts a level at all.

None of that is difficulty. It is cold start. You cannot fix it by making the puzzle easier, because they were not trying to solve the puzzle.

The second group is genuinely stuck, and they are much further in, at the point where the content first asks something real of you. Their median time on the last level they ever touch roughly doubles, and a third of them spend over two minutes on it before they go. That is difficulty churn, it responds to difficulty work, and it is a good deal smaller than the first group.

We spent a release tuning the opening for a population that was not failing it.

The one number with a shape worth acting on

Out of everything we looked at, one measurement behaved differently.

Take the first time a player is ever offered a rewarded video — in our case a rescue, triggered automatically when they stall rather than hunted for — and compare how often it is actually watched, by ordinal. The first one is far worse than the second. The second is worse than the third. It recovers from there, and keeps recovering.

The shape is the finding, not the level. A flat line would mean players simply dislike video ads, and the fix would be to show fewer of them. A line that starts low and climbs means the first one is a surprise, and the fix is something you do once per player: say what is about to happen before it happens, and never again.

That is a much cheaper intervention than rebuilding your ad strategy, and we would not have known to reach for it from the average.

The part where we hold it loosely

This reading is correlational too, and having just been caught by one correlation we are trying not to repeat the trick. Players who were already on their way out may tap and close, which would produce the same curve from the opposite direction.

What argues against that: it is the only placement in the game that fires unprompted on a single tap; its completion rate is anomalously low against other placements running on the same ad stack; and the monotonic recovery by ordinal is hard to explain if the cause is the kind of player rather than the moment. The high ordinals are badly survivorship-biased — a player on their eleventh rescue has self-selected as someone who tolerates rescues. The first-attempt row is the clean one, because every player appears in it exactly once.

So it is being tested rather than believed, and the test is a confirmation step, not a formality.

The generalisable bit

  • A threshold in a correlation describes your users. It is not a lever on them. Before building against one, find a release that already moved it and check whether the outcome moved too.
  • Split your churn before you diagnose it. Ours looked like one curve and was two populations with opposite causes. The larger one was invisible because it leaves before it does anything you thought to measure.
  • Prefer findings with a shape. “This number is bad” tells you to improve it. “This number is bad and then recovers” tells you what kind of fix it is. The second is worth far more and costs one extra group-by.

We kept the six-level work, for the record. The opening is better than it was, new players get further into it, and that is worth having on its own terms. It simply was not the retention fix, and we should not have needed the data to tell us twice.

The game all of this is about is free, and the opening is the part we have rebuilt the most.
